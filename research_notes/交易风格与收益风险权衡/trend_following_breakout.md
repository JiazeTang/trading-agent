## Q1. Time-series momentum (TSMOM) / trend following: MOP (2012), Hurst-Ooi-Pedersen (2017), Lempérière et al. (2014); the "smile" and crisis alpha

### Takeaway
Diversified, volatility-scaled trend following across ~58–67 futures markets has a long, statistically strong record: Sharpe above 1 gross over 1985–2009 (MOP), about 0.76 net of 2/20 fees and costs over 1880–2016 (HOP), and t-stat ≈10 over ~200 years (CFM). Returns form a "smile" against equity-market returns. They are best in large up and large down quarters and weakest when markets move little, and they were positive in 8 of the 10 worst 60/40 drawdowns since 1880. Post-2009 performance was much weaker (2010s "lull", a −20% SG Trend drawdown in 2024–25). The best-supported explanation is smaller market moves, not a broken signal.

### Cited Findings
**Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012), "Time series momentum", JFE 104(2):228–250**
- Universe: 58 liquid futures/forwards (equity indices, currencies, commodities, sovereign bonds). Main sample 1985–2009, with more than 25 years of data. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463); [Quantpedia summary (secondary, for 1985–2009)](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/time-series-momentum-effect/)
- Horizon: an asset's own past 12-month excess return predicts its next-month return. The effect persists for 1–12 months and partially reverses at longer horizons, which fits "initial under-reaction and delayed over-reaction". — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463); [NYU PDF](https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/facdir/lpederse/papers/TimeSeriesMomentum.pdf)
- Construction: each instrument is scaled to a 40% ex-ante annualized volatility target. Kim, Tse & Wald note that the average instrument volatility is ~19%, so this is roughly 2x leverage. — [Alpha Architect summary of Kim-Tse-Wald (secondary)](https://alphaarchitect.com/time-series-momentum-volatility-scaling-and-crisis-alpha/)
- Performance: the diversified TSMOM portfolio has "a Sharpe ratio greater than one on an annual basis, or roughly 2.5 times the Sharpe ratio for the equity market". The authors' slides put it at ~9–10% annualized volatility. Results are gross (no fees, no transaction costs in the headline). The paper also reports substantial abnormal returns with little exposure to standard factors (market, bonds, commodities, value/size/momentum). — [MOP paper PDF](https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/facdir/lpederse/papers/TimeSeriesMomentum.pdf); [MOP slides](http://docs.lhpedersen.com/TSMOM_Slides.pdf); [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463)
- Smile and extreme markets: plotted against quarterly equity-market returns, TSMOM quarterly returns form a "smile". The strategy "performs best during extreme markets", in both large up and large down moves. The abnormal returns "do not appear to be compensation for crash risk or tail events". — [MOP paper PDF](https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/facdir/lpederse/papers/TimeSeriesMomentum.pdf); [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463)
- Flow evidence: speculators appear to profit from TSMOM at the expense of hedgers (CFTC positioning data). — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463)
- Exact numbers not retrieved: the precise Sharpe of the diversified factor, its skewness, and the coefficient on squared market returns (see Gaps).

**Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen (2017), "A Century of Evidence on Trend-Following Investing", Journal of Portfolio Management 44(1):15–29**
- Universe and method: 67 markets (29 commodities, 11 equity indices, 15 bond markets, 12 currency pairs), Jan 1880–Dec 2016. Equal-weighted 1-, 3- and 12-month TSMOM, risk-scaled, with a 10% annualized portfolio volatility target. Results are net of simulated transaction costs and of 2% management / 20% performance fees. — [AQR page](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing); [2017 PDF copy](https://static.twentyoverten.com/593e8a9e7299b471eaecf644/SkLoGL67M/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing.pdf)
- Full-sample row of the performance table (Jan 1880–Dec 2016): **18.0% | 11.0% | 7.3% | 9.7% | 0.76 | −0.01 | −0.03**. The column headers were not visible in the excerpt. My reading is: gross return, net-of-fee return, net excess return, realized volatility, net Sharpe, correlation to S&P 500, correlation to US 10-year bonds. The arithmetic supports this reading, since 7.3/9.7 ≈ 0.75. The 1880s decade row is 12.1% | 5.2% | 2.6% | 9.5% | 0.27 | −0.11 | −0.04. — [2017 PDF copy](https://static.twentyoverten.com/593e8a9e7299b471eaecf644/SkLoGL67M/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing.pdf); [trendfollowing.com copy](https://www.trendfollowing.com/whitepaper/Century_Evidence_Trend_Following.pdf)
- The strategy had positive average returns in every decade from 1880 to 2016. — [AQR page](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing)
- Crisis alpha: the strategy was positive in 8 of the 10 worst 60/40 drawdowns (2017 edition), and those crises averaged ~15 months. The earlier edition, with data to 2013, reported 9 of 10. — [2017 PDF copy](https://static.twentyoverten.com/593e8a9e7299b471eaecf644/SkLoGL67M/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing.pdf); [trendfollowing.com copy (earlier edition)](https://www.trendfollowing.com/whitepaper/Century_Evidence_Trend_Following.pdf)
- Portfolio effect: a 20% TSMOM allocation added to 60/40 raised the Sharpe from 0.39 to 0.55 and cut the maximum drawdown from −62.3% to −50.2%. The 60/40 baseline itself had ~10.7–10.8% volatility, a −62.3% max drawdown and a Sharpe of 0.38–0.39. Its return figure differs between editions (7.8% vs 4.1%, possibly total vs excess return). — [2017 PDF copy](https://static.twentyoverten.com/593e8a9e7299b471eaecf644/SkLoGL67M/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing.pdf)
- Conflicting secondary figure: CXO Advisory says the strategy had an "0.4 average Sharpe ratio after 2%+20% fees (1880-2016; 67 assets)". This conflicts with the 0.76 table value. It may be an average across single-asset strategies; this was not resolved. — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/momentum-investing/trend-following-with-intrinsic-momentum-over-the-very-long-run)

**Lempérière, Deremble, Seager, Potters & Bouchaud (2014), "Two centuries of trend following", Journal of Investment Strategies (CFM)**
- Universe: commodities, currencies, stock indices and bonds, back to ~1800. The signal is a ~5-month moving-average trend. The Sharpe is computed without subtracting the risk-free rate, because futures are self-financed. — [arXiv 1404.3274](https://arxiv.org/abs/1404.3274); [CFM page](https://www.cfm.com/two-centuries-of-trend-following/)
- Significance: the excess-return t-stat is ≈5 since 1960 and ≈10 since 1800, after controlling for markets' upward drift. For the 5-month strategy on futures since 1960, the t-stat is 5.9 (Sharpe ≈ 0.8) and the de-biased t-stat is 5.0. The authors call the effect "remarkably steady over two centuries" and "one of the most statistically significant anomalies". — [arXiv 1404.3274](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1404.3274)
- A secondary summary gives t = 10.5, de-biased t = 9.8, Sharpe = 0.72, but does not state which sample period these cover. — [Return Stacked review (secondary)](https://www.returnstacked.com/academic-review/two-centuries-of-trend-following/)
- On the recent plateau: the paper argues that the post-2011 flat period is not unusual, because strategies with Sharpe below ~0.8 can have long drawdowns, and that crowding is not the only explanation. — [arXiv 1404.3274](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1404.3274)

**Crisis alpha (other primary)**
- Hamill, Rattray & Van Hemert (Man AHL, 2016), "Trend following: equity and bond crisis alpha": TSMOM in bonds, commodities, currencies and equity indices, 1960–2015. Performance was "particularly strong in the worst equity and bond market environments". Returns are positively skewed, consistent with a parallel to a long straddle. Barring long-equity and long-bond positions further improves crisis alpha but lowers the average return. — [SSRN 2831926](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2831926)

**Post-publication decay / recent evidence**
- Kim, Tse & Wald (2016): MOP's results are driven largely by volatility scaling (risk parity), not by the time-series signal. The outperformance of TSMOM "vanishes in the more recent post-crisis period of 2009 to 2013". — [Alpha Architect summary (secondary)](https://alphaarchitect.com/time-series-momentum-volatility-scaling-and-crisis-alpha/)
- Huang, Li, Wang & Zhou (2020), "Time series momentum: Is it there?", JFE 135(3):774–794: asset-by-asset regressions show "little TSM evidence, both in-sample and out-of-sample". The large pooled-regression t-stat falls below bootstrap critical values. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v135y2020i3p774-794.html); [SMU repository](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=7520&context=lkcsb_research)
- Babu, Hoffman, Levine, Ooi, Schroeder & Stamelos (2020), "You Can't Always Trend When You Want", JPM 46(4):52: weak 2010s trend returns are explained mainly by smaller average market moves. The strategy's ability to profit from a given move, and diversification across markets, held up. The authors find little evidence of a permanent structural change. — [AQR page](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/You-Cant-Always-Trend-When-You-Want); [PM Research](https://www.pm-research.com/content/iijpormgmt/46/4/52)
- SG Trend Index (practitioner benchmark, net of fees):
  - 2022: +27.4%. — [Cambridge Associates](https://www.cambridgeassociates.com/insight/does-trend-followings-recent-struggle-signal-that-the-strategy-is-structurally-broken/)
  - May 2024–May 2025: −20.4%, the second-largest drawdown since the index began in 2000. The index has had 16 drawdowns larger than 10% since 2000. — [Cambridge Associates](https://www.cambridgeassociates.com/insight/does-trend-followings-recent-struggle-signal-that-the-strategy-is-structurally-broken/)
  - Rolling 12-month return of −18.6% to 30 Apr 2025. A 12-month loss greater than 15% has happened only twice. — [Man Group](https://www.man.com/insights/is-this-time-different)
  - 12-month trailing return −15.05% at June 2025, with a 20.61% max drawdown. — [Top Traders Unplugged](https://www.toptradersunplugged.com/trend-following-performance-report-june-2025/)
- Kurth, Eisler, Rej & Bouchaud (arXiv 2607.01550, July 2026), "Is Trend Still Your Friend? A Microstructural Account of the Demise of Short-Term Trend-Following": ~100 liquid futures, 1995–2025. After 2008, trend P&L fell sharply on contracts with small volatility-normalized tick size, across signal horizons, but held up on large-tick contracts. The proposed mechanism is liquidity offered to trend followers drying up. Alpha Architect notes that the HFT link is not directly tested. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01550); [Alpha Architect commentary](https://alphaarchitect.com/short-term-trend-following/)

### Inferences
- The smile means a trend strategy's P&L depends mostly on how large the moves in the period are, not on their direction. In the user's regime split, trend/breakout should earn most in strongly up or strongly down quarters and least, often negative after costs, in sideways quarters. A long-only QQQ breakout only collects the up-side of the smile. In down quarters it sits in cash at best (no crisis alpha) and is whipsawed during bear-market rallies.
- MOP's Sharpe >1 is gross and diversified across 58 markets. HOP's 0.76 is net and diversified across 67. A single-instrument trend rule on one equity index gets none of that diversification. Expect single-asset Sharpe well below these figures, consistent with CXO's "0.4" figure if that is a per-asset average (not verified).
- The 2010s weakness (Babu et al.) and the 2024–25 drawdown are consistent with "low realized moves → poor trend returns". A recent run of poor breakout results in a range-bound market is what the literature predicts. On its own it is not evidence that the signal has stopped working.
- Kurth et al. (2026) is relevant to QQQ. A $0.01 tick on a ~$400–600 ETF is a very small tick relative to volatility, which is the category where they found post-2008 trend decay (my inference; they studied futures, not ETFs).

### Gaps
- MOP: the exact Sharpe of the diversified factor (I recall roughly 1.3–1.6 but could not verify it), its annual return, skewness, max drawdown, and the coefficient on squared market returns. The paper PDF was blocked.
- HOP: column headers for the 0.76 row could not be confirmed, and the CXO "0.4 Sharpe" conflict is unresolved. Per-crisis returns were not retrieved.
- Lempérière 2014: the full-sample Sharpe since 1800 (0.72 secondary vs ~0.8 for post-1960 futures) is not pinned down.
- SG Trend Index calendar-year returns for 2023, 2024 and 2025 were not reliably sourced. One excerpt said about −3.3% for 2023, but its source was unclear.
- Win rate and payoff ratio are not reported in MOP, HOP or Lempérière (see Q2 for practitioner numbers).

## Q2. Why trend following resembles a long straddle / long volatility (Fung & Hsieh 2001), and what that implies for sideways markets

### Takeaway
Fung & Hsieh model a trend follower as holding lookback straddles. The payoff is max−min of the price over the period, which is convex and long volatility. Like a long option, it bleeds premium (whipsaw losses) when prices move sideways and pays off in large moves of either sign. This produces positive skew, a hit rate below 50%, large winners and many small losers. Positive skew is documented in several primary sources. Hit-rate and payoff numbers come only from practitioner sources.

### Cited Findings
- Fung & Hsieh (2001), "The risk in hedge fund strategies: theory and evidence from trend followers", RFS 14(2):313–341. Trend-following fund returns are modeled with lookback straddles, which pay "the difference between the highest and lowest price of the reference asset" over the period, like a trend follower with perfect foresight. These "explain trend-following funds' returns better than standard asset indices". Standard straddles give similar empirical results, but lookback straddles are theoretically closer to trend following. — [SSRN 250542](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=250542); [paper PDF copy](https://www.trendfollowing.com/whitepaper/hsieh_fung_final_paper.pdf)
- The PTFS (primitive trend-following strategy) factors are lookback-straddle portfolios on bond, currency, commodity, short-term interest-rate and stock-index futures. They later became part of the standard Fung-Hsieh hedge-fund factor model. — [paper PDF copy](https://www.trendfollowing.com/whitepaper/hsieh_fung_final_paper.pdf); [Duke working paper](https://people.duke.edu/~dah7/TheRiskinHedgeFundStrategies.pdf)
- Earlier evidence (Fung & Hsieh 1997, recounted in the 2001 paper): trend-following fund returns tend to be large and positive in both the best and the worst months of world equity markets, and are uncorrelated with standard asset indices. This is the empirical origin of the "smile". — [paper PDF copy](https://www.trendfollowing.com/whitepaper/hsieh_fung_final_paper.pdf)
- Hamill, Rattray & Van Hemert (2016): trend returns are positively skewed, "which we argue is intuitive by drawing a parallel between momentum strategies and a long option straddle strategy" (1960–2015, multi-asset futures). — [SSRN 2831926](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2831926)
- Adding TSMOM factors to the Fung-Hsieh model raises its explanatory power for managed-futures returns, and some lookback-straddle factors lose significance. TSMOM and lookback straddles capture overlapping risk. — [Erdos & Elaut, "Time Series Momentum: Benchmarking the Managed Futures Industry"](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/29806/PErdos-GElaut_Time-series-momentum.pdf)
- Whipsaw mechanism (Zakamulin): moving-average rules give correct signals, with a lag, in trending markets. "When prices trend sideways, moving average rules tend to generate many false signals." These whipsaw trades "deteriorate the profitability of a trading strategy". Using two moving averages (crossover) is one way to reduce them. — [Alpha Architect, Zakamulin series Part 3](https://alphaarchitect.com/trend-following-valeriy-zakamulin-technical-trading-rules-part-3/)
- Practitioner hit rate and payoff (secondary, not peer-reviewed): medium-term trend CTAs have hit rates of ~35–40%, with winners ~2–3x the size of losers. Short-term CTAs hit ~50–60% with winners and losers of similar size. — [Advisor Analyst, "A Tale of Two CTAs" (Aug 2026)](https://advisoranalyst.com/2026/08/13/a-tale-of-two-ctas.html/); [Tallus Wealth Management](https://talluswealthmanagement.com/2026/08/12/a-tale-of-two-ctas-short-term-vs-trend-following/)
- Range-bound, whipsawing conditions are "generally unfavourable to CTAs" because they need persistent trends. — [The Hedge Fund Journal](https://thehedgefundjournal.com/back-to-the-futures/)

### Inferences
- In long-option terms, a sideways market is time decay. Each false breakout costs roughly the stop distance plus spread and slippage, and nothing pays it back until a large move arrives. For the user's QQQ breakout system, the expected profile is negative returns in sideways quarters, and a P&L dominated by a few large winning trends. The equity curve should also show long flat or declining stretches even when the strategy has a real edge.
- A win rate of ~25–40% with a payoff ratio above 2 is the normal signature of a working breakout/trend system, not by itself a sign of failure. The things to check are whether average win / average loss stays above (1 − p)/p, and whether the losses are concentrated in low-realized-volatility regimes.
- Because the strategy is long convexity, a fair comparison with mean-reversion styles should weigh skew and drawdown shape, not only Sharpe (see Q3).

### Gaps
- Fung & Hsieh's exact R² values and the extreme-month tables were not retrieved (PDF blocked).
- I found no peer-reviewed study reporting trade-level win rates and payoff ratios for systematic trend following. The 35–40% / 2–3x figures are practitioner estimates.
- I found no study that measures whipsaw cost specifically in "sideways quarters" with the user's regime definition. The closest are the smile chart (MOP) and Babu et al.'s move-size decomposition.

## Q3. Skewness vs Sharpe across strategy styles: Lempérière et al. (2017), "Risk premia: asymmetric tail risks and excess returns"

### Takeaway
Across many classic risk premia (equity, Fama-French factors, FX carry, short volatility, bonds, credit), Sharpe ratio rises roughly linearly with negative skewness. This is consistent with premia being payment for tail risk. Trend following stands out with positive skew and positive Sharpe, so the authors classify it as a market anomaly (likely behavioural) rather than a risk premium. The usual trade-off is to accept negative skew for higher Sharpe; trend is the main documented exception.

### Cited Findings
- Lempérière, Deremble, Nguyen, Seager, Potters & Bouchaud (2017), Quantitative Finance (DOI 10.1080/14697688.2016.1183035; arXiv 1409.7720): "risk premium" is "strongly correlated with tail-risk skewness but very little with volatility". There is "an approximately linear relation between the Sharpe ratio of various risk premium strategies (Equity, Fama-French, FX Carry, Short Vol, Bonds, Credit) and their negative skewness". — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14697688.2016.1183035); [arXiv 1409.7720](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.7720)
- Exception: "trend following (and perhaps the Fama-French 'High minus Low')… has positive skewness and positive excess returns, suggesting that some strategies are not risk premia but genuine market anomalies." — [arXiv 1409.7720](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.7720); [SSRN 2502743](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2502743)
- Fitted "skew-rewarding" line: S ≈ 1/3 − ζ*/4, where ζ* is the authors' low-moment skewness measure, not the standard third moment. The 2017 update note on option strangles gives intercept a ≈ 0.3 and slope b ≈ 0.17, close to the earlier values. — [arXiv 1708.07637 update](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.07637); [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14697688.2016.1183035)
- Trend-following skewness: daily ζ* = +0.43, and monthly skewness is even more positive (ζ*_month = +1.72). — [arXiv 1708.07637 update](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.07637)
- Correlation of risk premium with volatility is "negative or zero" in most cases, while correlation with skew is "chiefly negative". — [arXiv 1708.07637 update](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.07637)
- Cross-check from a different team (Baz, Granger, Harvey, Le Roux & Rattray, "Dissecting Investment Strategies in the Cross Section and Time Series"): momentum has "substantially better risk return characteristics in time series than in cross section". Value and momentum are positively skewed and carry is negatively skewed. — [CME-hosted PDF](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/files/dissecting-investment-strategies-in-the-cross-section-and-time-series.pdf)

### Inferences
- When the project weighs styles by return/risk, Sharpe alone will favour negatively skewed styles: mean reversion, short volatility, buying dips in an uptrend. Those can look excellent in calm, range-bound quarters and then lose heavily in a crash. Trend/breakout accepts a lower or lumpier Sharpe in exchange for positive skew and crisis convexity. Reporting skewness, max drawdown, and worst-quarter return per regime alongside Sharpe would make that trade-off visible.
- A plausible prior from this literature: mean-reversion-style QQQ strategies will win in sideways quarters, and breakout will win in large-move quarters (especially down quarters, if shorting is allowed). Combining the two diversifies across the smile.

### Gaps
- The CFM paper's per-strategy table (Sharpe and ζ* for each premium, sample periods) was not retrieved. Only the fitted line and the trend skew values are verified.
- The skewness measure is non-standard, so values are not directly comparable to a standard third-moment skewness computed on the user's backtest returns.

## Q4. Trend/breakout rules on a single equity index: Brock-Lakonishok-LeBaron (1992), Faber (2007), Zakamulin (2014–2018), and data-snooping follow-ups

### Takeaway
On a single US equity index, long-horizon trend timing (10-month SMA, 200-day MA, N-day breakouts) mainly cuts volatility and drawdowns: Faber reports 17.9% → 12.0% volatility and a −83.7% → −42.2% drawdown in 1929–32, with similar long-run return. Most of the benefit comes from avoiding a few major bear markets. In bull and sideways markets it gives up return through lag and whipsaws. BLL's daily-rule "profits" (1897–1986, gross) did not hold up out-of-sample after 1986. Once data-snooping is controlled for and realistic costs added, the excess return over buy-and-hold is statistically weak or zero (Sullivan-Timmermann-White 1999; Bajgrowicz-Scaillet 2012; Zakamulin).

### Cited Findings
**Brock, Lakonishok & LeBaron (1992), "Simple Technical Trading Rules and the Stochastic Properties of Stock Returns", JF 47:1731–1764**
- DJIA daily, 1897–1986. 26 rules: variable-length and fixed-length moving averages (VMA, FMA) and trading-range breakouts (TRB). The breakout rules buy when price exceeds the maximum of the past 50, 150 or 200 days. Inference used bootstrap methods. — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1992.tb04681.x); [Alpha Architect summary (secondary)](https://alphaarchitect.com/old-school-academics-on-moving-average-rules-remarkable/)
- Returns after buy signals were higher and less volatile than returns after sell signals, and returns after sell signals were negative. — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1992.tb04681.x); [Brandeis repository](https://scholarworks.brandeis.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Simple-Technical-Trading-Rules-and-the/9924036588601921)
- The returns were inconsistent with random walk, AR(1), GARCH-M and EGARCH null models. — UNVERIFIED wording (recalled from the abstract; the abstract page was not fetchable) — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1992.tb04681.x)
- Costs were not modeled (gross). LeBaron's follow-up finds the 150-day MA among the most consistent rules. — [LeBaron, "The Stability of Moving Average Technical Trading Rules on the Dow Jones Index"](https://people.brandeis.edu/~blebaron/wps/dowvoltt.pdf)
- The commonly quoted figures (≈+12%/yr after buy signals vs ≈−7%/yr after sell signals) could not be verified. — UNVERIFIED

**Data-snooping and out-of-sample follow-ups**
- Sullivan, Timmermann & White (1999), JF 54:1647–1691: White's Reality Check on an expanded universe of 7,846 rules over ~100 years of DJIA data. BLL's 1897–1986 result survives the snooping adjustment. However, the best rule's significant performance "is not realised" out-of-sample on the DJIA in 1987–1996 or on S&P 500 futures (the 1987–96 claim is reported second-hand). — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/0022-1082.00163); [Kevin Sheppard-hosted PDF](https://www.kevinsheppard.com/files/teaching/mfe/advanced-econometrics/Sullivan_Timmermann_White.pdf)
- Bajgrowicz & Scaillet (2012), "Technical trading revisited: False discoveries, persistence tests, and transaction costs", JFE: the same 7,846 rules on the DJIA, Jan 1897–Jul 2011, with a false-discovery-rate control. An investor "would never have been able to select ex ante the future best-performing rules". "Even in-sample, the performance is completely offset by the introduction of low transaction costs." — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X1200116X); [UNIGE repository](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:79889)

**Faber (2007; 2013 update), "A Quantitative Approach to Tactical Asset Allocation", Journal of Wealth Management**
- Rule: hold the S&P 500 when the month-end price is above its 10-month SMA, otherwise hold cash (T-bills). Signals are checked monthly. Taxes, commissions and slippage are excluded (gross). The strategy is invested ~70% of the time. — [Faber SSRN PDF](https://mebfaber.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/SSRN-id962461.pdf)
- 1901–2012: CAGR 10.18% for timing vs 9.32% for buy-and-hold. Volatility 12.0% vs 17.9%. — [Faber SSRN PDF](https://mebfaber.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/SSRN-id962461.pdf); [CXO Advisory summary of the 2013 update (secondary for volatility)](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/long-term-outperformance-from-trends-defined-by-moving-averages/)
- Drawdowns:
  - 1929–32 episode: timing cut the drawdown "from a catastrophic 83.66% to a more manageable 42.24%".
  - 2000–02: 16.52% for timing vs 44.73% for buy-and-hold.
  - The rule exited on 31 Dec 2007, avoiding the 2008–09 bear market (50.95% drawdown).
  - Drawdowns are measured on monthly data.
  — [Faber SSRN PDF](https://mebfaber.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/SSRN-id962461.pdf)
- Faber's own caveat: the outperformance comes largely from avoiding most of the drawdowns of major bear markets. He tested 3–12-month MA lengths to address possible optimization. — [Faber SSRN PDF](https://mebfaber.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/SSRN-id962461.pdf)

**Zakamulin (2014–2018)**
- "The Real-Life Performance of Market Timing with Moving Average and Time-Series Momentum Rules" (SSRN 2242795; J. Asset Management 2014): reported performance of MA/TSMOM timing "usually contains a considerable data-mining bias and ignores important market frictions". In out-of-sample tests with realistic transaction costs, performance is "highly overstated". — [SSRN](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.2242795)
- In the longest out-of-sample test of these rules (S&P Composite, 140 years, 1870–2009), there is no single optimal lookback. The optimal length is time-varying and probably depends on market conditions. — [Zakamulin SSRN PDF](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/13144853/SSRN-moving-average.pdf)
- S&P 500 tests in his Alpha Architect series: 0.25% one-way costs, in-sample period ending 1950, and a long out-of-sample period. The p-value of the outperformance test lies between 15% and 25% for most choices, which is weak statistical support. — [Alpha Architect Part 7](https://alphaarchitect.com/trend-following-valeriy-zakamulin-trading-sp-500-index-part-7/)
- "Revisiting the Profitability of Market Timing with Moving Averages" (International Review of Finance 18:317–327, 2018): the "too good to be true" results of an earlier study came from look-ahead bias. Without it, the MA strategy is "at best… only marginally better" than buy-and-hold and statistically indistinguishable from it. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/irfi.12132); [SSRN 2743119](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2743119)
- Whipsaws: MA rules generate many false signals in sideways markets (see Q2). — [Alpha Architect Part 3](https://alphaarchitect.com/trend-following-valeriy-zakamulin-technical-trading-rules-part-3/)

### Inferences
- For a single index, the robust benefit of trend rules is risk reduction: lower volatility and much shallower crash drawdowns, at a cost of trading lag. Robust return enhancement is not supported. In a strongly rising market like QQQ 2009–2021 or 2023–24, expect timing to lag buy-and-hold because of exits on corrections that recover. In sideways quarters, expect repeated small losses from whipsaws. In deep down quarters (2000–02, 2008, 2022), expect large relative gains.
- Signal frequency matters a great deal. BLL/STW-style daily rules died after 1986 and were wiped out by low costs. Monthly rules like Faber's trade rarely, so costs matter less. A 30-minute-bar breakout trades far more often than either, so it is the most exposed to the cost and data-snooping failure described by Bajgrowicz-Scaillet.
- When the user tests many breakout parameters, the false-discovery problem applies directly. Report how many variants were tried, and use a holdout or FDR/Reality-Check-style correction.

### Gaps
- BLL's exact buy/sell annualized returns, standard deviations and t-stats were not retrieved.
- Faber's full-period max drawdown for the timing model (1901–2012) was not verified. Only the 1929–32 (42.24%) and 2000–02 (16.52%) episode figures were.
- I could not retrieve a primary source that quantifies, for Zakamulin's S&P 500 tests, the outperformance split by bull vs bear vs sideways periods. The bear-market concentration rests on Faber's own statement.
- I found no peer-reviewed study of MA/breakout timing specifically on the Nasdaq-100/QQQ (daily or monthly).

## Q5. Cross-sectional momentum crashes (Daniel & Moskowitz 2016) vs time-series trend risk

### Takeaway
Cross-sectional (winners-minus-losers, WML) equity momentum is strongly negatively skewed: −4.70 monthly over 1927–2013, with crashes of −91.6% in two months in 1932 and −73.4% in three months in 2009. Crashes come in "panic states" right after bear markets, when volatility is high and the market rebounds sharply, because the short leg (past losers) becomes high-beta and option-like. Diversified time-series trend is instead positively skewed and tends to profit in sustained crises. However, it is also hurt by sharp V-shaped reversals and can have deep drawdowns.

### Cited Findings
- Daniel & Moskowitz (2016), "Momentum crashes", JFE 122:221–247 (NBER w20439). Crashes occur "following market declines, when market volatility is high, and are contemporaneous with market rebounds". They are partly forecastable. Low expected returns in panic states reflect a high premium on the option-like payoff of past losers. A dynamic strategy that forecasts momentum's mean and variance "approximately doubles the alpha and Sharpe ratio" of static momentum. — [JFE PDF (author site)](https://www.kentdaniel.net/papers/published/jfe_16.pdf); [NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf); [Columbia page](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/momentum-crashes)
- Crash sizes: WML lost −91.59% over July–August 1932 and −73.42% over March–May 2009. These are as cited in Barroso & Santa-Clara and Bianchi et al.; other constructions give −88.1%/−67.4% or −88.5%/−45.6%. — [Barroso & Santa-Clara, "Momentum has its moments", JFE](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X14002566); [Bianchi, De Polis & Petrella, "Taming Momentum Crashes"](https://www.oru.se/globalassets/oru-en/schools/hh/financial-econometrics-2022/bianchi-tamingmomentumcrashes.pdf)
- What drove the crashes: in July–Aug 1932 the past-loser decile returned +232% vs +32% for past winners. In Mar–May 2009 losers gained +163% vs +8% for winners. — [Daniel & Moskowitz 2011 draft (NYU)](https://www.stern.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/assets/documents/con_038332.pdf)
- Skewness (working-paper version): WML monthly (daily) skewness is −4.70 (−1.18). The winner decile is −0.82 (−0.61) and the loser decile 0.09 (0.12). — [Daniel & Moskowitz 2013 draft (Ivey-hosted)](https://www.ivey.uwo.ca/media/3775547/momentumcrashes.pdf)
- Persistence of damage: a dollar in momentum in July 1932 was not recovered until April 1963 (Barroso & Santa-Clara's calculation). — [Butt, "Momentum crashes and variations to market liquidity"](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/633394/1/Momentum%20crashes%20IJFE.pdf)
- Time-series momentum can also crash. Liu, Lu & Wang note that TSM is "prone to deep and persistent drawdowns similar to cross-sectional momentum crashes". — [Liu, Lu & Wang, "Asymmetry, tail risk and time series momentum", IRFA](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057521921002581)
- In contrast, MOP find TSMOM is not compensation for crash risk and performs best in extreme markets. CFM measures trend skew as positive (daily ζ* +0.43, monthly +1.72). — [MOP SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463); [arXiv 1708.07637](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.07637)

### Inferences
- The two styles share a name but have opposite tail shapes. Cross-sectional momentum is a long-short bet on relative winners whose short leg turns into a short call in panics: negative skew, worst at bear-market bottoms. A single-asset time-series breakout is a directional bet whose worst case is the V-shaped reversal, such as QQQ in March–April 2020 or the April 2025 tariff shock and rebound (examples are my inference, not from sources). In a V-reversal, a trend system that went short on the way down is stopped out on the rebound, which is a smaller version of the "panic-state rebound" mechanism.
- For the user's down-quarter bucket, short-horizon breakout can do well in persistent declines (2022-type) but poorly in quarters that fall and then snap back. Splitting down quarters by "trend persistence" (e.g., the ratio of net quarterly move to sum of absolute daily moves) would separate these cases.

### Gaps
- Final-JFE-version figures for WML skewness and Sharpe (sample 1927–2013) were not checked against the published tables. The numbers above are from working-paper drafts.
- I found no source that directly compares the skewness of single-index time-series breakout strategies with cross-sectional momentum on the same sample.

## Q6. Intraday / short-horizon breakout on equity-index ETFs (SPY/QQQ): Zarattini-Aziz-Barbon (2024) and related evidence

### Takeaway
The most directly relevant paper is Zarattini, Aziz & Barbon (2024). On SPY over 2007 to early 2024 it reports a net total return of 1,985%, 19.6% per year, and a Sharpe of 1.33. Its rules: a "noise area" breakout checked every 30 minutes, confirmation by VWAP, a trailing stop, volatility-targeted sizing up to 4x, and flat at the close. It is a full-sample backtest by practitioners (Concretum / Bear Bull Traders), with no peer-reviewed out-of-sample test and, as far as I found, no QQQ test in the paper. Independent replications get a Sharpe of ~1.1 in-sample, and one reports the edge near zero since 2025. Academic intraday-momentum evidence (Gao et al. 2018; Baltussen et al. 2021) supports a real but small effect, tied to volatile days and options gamma hedging. The QQQ 5-minute opening-range-breakout paper replicates gross but breaks even at about 2¢/share slippage.

### Cited Findings
**Zarattini, Aziz & Barbon (2024; revised 2025), "Beat the Market: An Effective Intraday Momentum Strategy for S&P500 ETF (SPY)", SSRN 4824172**
- Headline: "From 2007 to early 2024, the resulting intraday momentum portfolio achieved a total return of 1,985% (net of costs), an annualized return of 19.6%, and a Sharpe Ratio of 1.33." Unlike studies that trade only the last 30 minutes, trades are started from intraday demand/supply imbalances, using day-trader techniques and dynamic trailing stops. — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4824172); [Concretum Group page](https://concretumgroup.com/beat-the-market-an-effective-intraday-momentum-strategy-for-sp500-etf-spy/); [University of St. Gallen record](https://alexandria.unisg.ch/entities/publication/71ef0a23-34e3-4b37-a8d7-d98285579662)
- Rules, as described by reviewers and replicators (secondary):
  - The "Noise Area" is built from SPY's average absolute move from the open at each time of day over the previous 14 sessions.
  - The upper and lower boundaries are anchored to the max/min of today's open and yesterday's close, to handle overnight gaps.
  - Signals are checked every 30 minutes.
  - Long if price is above the upper boundary and above VWAP; short if below the lower boundary and below VWAP; otherwise flat.
  - Trailing stop uses the boundary/VWAP.
  - All positions are closed by 16:00.
  - Sizing uses daily volatility targeting (a 2% daily-volatility target in one replication) with a 4x maximum-leverage cap.
  — [QuantMacro paper review](https://quantmacro.substack.com/p/paper-review-an-effective-intraday); [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/momentum-investing/complex-intraday-time-series-momentum-strategy-applied-to-spy/); [Concretum Substack](https://concretumgroup.substack.com/p/beat-the-market-with-intraday-momentum)
- Out-of-sample and other instruments: the reported result covers the full 2007–2024 sample. I found no holdout period and no QQQ test in the paper, in its abstract or in summaries (see Gaps).
- Independent replication (codecat-ops, GitHub; not peer-reviewed): Sharpe 1.11, alpha +16.7%/yr (t = 2.85), beta ≈ 0, against the paper's 1.33 / ~19.6%. "Edge compressed since 2025 | recent Sharpe ≈ 0 on both instruments (SPY and ES)." — [GitHub replication](https://github.com/codecat-ops/zarattini-2024-momentum-spy)
- Other follow-ups: Maróy (SSRN 5095349) studies parameter optimization and alternative exits. Another replication on 30-minute SPY bars trains on 2016–2021 and tests on 2022 to Oct 2026 (results not retrieved). — [Maróy SSRN 5095349](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5095349); [svettelnova replication](https://github.com/svettelnova/intraday-momentum-spy)

**Zarattini & Aziz (2023), "Can Day Trading Really Be Profitable? … Opening Range Breakout (ORB) …", SSRN 4416622 (QQQ, 5-minute ORB, 2016–2023)**
- Independent replication on QQQ: 1,775 trades (paper: 1,795) and Sharpe 1.06 (paper: 1.12). Break-even at ~2.2¢/share slippage. An NQ-futures confirmation filter is significant per trade (t = 2.05), but 76% of its P&L comes from 2022 alone. — [GitHub replication (giovannibrusco)](https://github.com/giovannibrusco/zarattini-2023-orb-qqq)
- A replication on five indices reports "gross reproduced, net zero". It also notes that the paper charges commission but no spread or slippage, and has no out-of-sample period. — [MQL5 blog, Sept 2026 (secondary)](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/776235); [danfin.net review (secondary)](https://danfin.net/opening-range-breakout-research)
- Secondary summaries of the paper's own QQQ figures: ~24% hit rate, about +0.13R per trade, ~33%/yr with 4x leverage, "+675%" net growth. These were not verified against the paper and are mutually inconsistent across summaries. — [CXO Advisory (secondary)](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/day-trading-with-an-opening-range-breakout-strategy/); [danfin.net (secondary)](https://danfin.net/opening-range-breakout-research)
- Separate paper, not QQQ: Zarattini, Barbon & Aziz (2024), "A Profitable Day Trading Strategy for the U.S. Equity Market". ORB on "Stocks in Play" across 7,000+ US stocks, 2016–2023. Sharpe 2.81 and 36% annualized alpha for a top-20 portfolio. — [SSRN 4729284](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4729284)

**Academic intraday-momentum evidence on index ETFs/futures**
- Gao, Han, Li & Zhou (2018), "Market intraday momentum", JFE 129:394–414. SPY, 1993–2013. The first half-hour return (measured from the prior close) predicts the last half-hour return, with R² 1.6% (working-paper version). The effect is stronger on volatile days, high-volume days, recession days and some macro-news days. A timing strategy based on it averages 6.67% per year with 6.19% standard deviation, a Sharpe of 1.08 vs 0.29 for buy-and-hold. These are full-sample figures. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X18301351); [working-paper PDF](https://assets.super.so/e46b77e7-ee08-445e-b43f-4ffd88ae0a0e/files/ee7dac49-530b-4950-b5d0-e0b5eee08f2e.pdf)
- A QuantConnect ETF replication of Gao et al. finds lower returns over its own test period. — [QuantConnect](https://www.quantconnect.com/research/15348/intraday-etf-momentum/)
- Baltussen, Da, Lammers & Martens (2021), "Hedging demand and market intraday momentum", JFE 142:377–403. Intraday momentum appears across 60+ futures (equities, bonds, commodities, FX) over 1974–2020. The mechanism is gamma hedging by short-gamma option market makers and leveraged-ETF rebalancing, both of which trade in the direction of the day's move. The paper reports out-of-sample trading-strategy relevance. — [SSRN 3760365](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3760365); [author PDF](https://academicweb.nd.edu/~zda/intramom.pdf)
- Short-term trend decay: Kurth, Eisler, Rej & Bouchaud (2026) find post-2008 trend P&L collapsed on small-tick contracts (see Q1). — [arXiv 2607.01550](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01550)

### Inferences
- Zarattini et al.'s design is a close template for a QQQ 30-minute breakout. It uses 30-minute decision points, a volatility-scaled noise boundary instead of a fixed range, VWAP confirmation, a trailing stop, and no overnight risk. Its documented strengths, however, are in-sample, on SPY, with leverage up to 4x. Reported Sharpe ~1.1–1.3 before 2024 and ~0 in 2025+ (one replication) suggests strong regime dependence or decay.
- The academic mechanisms (Gao: stronger on volatile and high-volume days; Baltussen: driven by gamma hedging) imply that intraday breakout P&L depends on intraday realized volatility and dealer gamma positioning, not on the quarter's direction. A "sideways quarter" with calm, mean-reverting days is close to the worst case: long-gamma dealers dampen moves, and noise-boundary breakouts fail. A sideways quarter with violent two-way days (e.g., tariff-headline whipsaws) may still pay. Tagging each day by realized intraday range/ATR, VIX level and, if available, dealer gamma sign may explain breakout P&L better than the up/sideways/down quarter label.
- Costs decide the outcome. The QQQ ORB edge vanishes at ~2.2¢/share slippage, and Bajgrowicz-Scaillet show low costs wipe out daily DJIA rules. A 30-minute QQQ backtest should model the spread and at least 1–2¢/share of slippage per side, and report results gross and net.

- The codecat-ops "recent Sharpe ≈ 0" claim comes from the repository description, and its method was not reviewed (GitHub access was outside this session's allowed repos).
- I found no peer-reviewed out-of-sample study of 30-minute breakout strategies on QQQ specifically.
- Gao et al.'s out-of-sample R² and results after 2013, and Baltussen et al.'s strategy Sharpe ratios, were not retrieved.
